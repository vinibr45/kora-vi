# Commercial Proposal Structure

## Summary

A commercial proposal should help a buyer understand the situation, recommended solution, scope, value, investment, timeline, terms, and next step.

## Core Idea

A strong proposal is a decision document, not just a price document.

Recommended structure:

1. Title and context.
2. Executive summary.
3. Buyer situation.
4. Objectives.
5. Recommended solution.
6. Scope and deliverables.
7. Process and timeline.
8. Investment.
9. Proof, assumptions, or credentials.
10. Terms, exclusions, and responsibilities.
11. Next step.

## When To Use

- Creating service proposals, project proposals, PDF offers, scopes of work, or sales follow-up documents.
- Turning a discovery call into a clear recommendation.
- Reviewing whether a proposal is buyer-friendly.

## When Not To Use

- When the document is a legally binding contract and requires legal review.
- When the buyer only needs a simple quote.
- When discovery has not clarified the buyer situation.

## How To Apply

Before writing, gather:

```text
Buyer:
Problem:
Desired outcome:
Decision criteria:
Recommended offer:
Deliverables:
Timeline:
Investment:
Responsibilities:
Risks or assumptions:
Next step:
```

Then write the proposal in buyer language, not only internal service language.

## Examples

Weak proposal section:

```text
Website development: R$ X.
```

Better proposal section:

```text
Recommended solution: a conversion-focused service website designed to explain the offer, build trust, and direct qualified visitors to WhatsApp.
```

## Limitations

Commercial proposals can create expectations and legal exposure. Sensitive terms, guarantees, payment rules, and contracts should be reviewed by qualified professionals when needed.
