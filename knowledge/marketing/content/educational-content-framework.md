# Educational Content Framework

## Summary

Educational content helps an audience understand a problem, compare options, make better decisions, and trust the source before buying.

It is especially useful when the buyer does not yet know what to ask, what to avoid, or how to evaluate a solution.

## Core Idea

Strong educational content does more than share tips. It changes how the audience sees the problem.

Useful educational content usually does one of five jobs:

1. Explain a concept.
2. Diagnose a problem.
3. Compare alternatives.
4. Show a process.
5. Prevent a mistake.

## When To Use

- Building authority in a market.
- Warming up leads before an offer.
- Creating posts, articles, PDFs, videos, carousels, email sequences, or training materials.
- Turning internal expertise into reusable public communication.

## When Not To Use

- When the audience already understands the problem and needs an offer.
- When urgency requires direct sales communication.
- When education becomes a way to avoid making a clear offer.

## How To Apply

Choose one job per piece of content:

```text
Concept: "What is this?"
Diagnosis: "How do I know this is my problem?"
Comparison: "Which option makes sense?"
Process: "How does this work step by step?"
Mistake prevention: "What should I avoid?"
```

Then structure the content:

1. Name the situation.
2. Explain why it matters.
3. Teach the core idea.
4. Give an example.
5. Show the next practical step.

## Examples

Concept post:

```text
What positioning is and why a beautiful website can still fail without it.
```

Diagnosis post:

```text
Five signs your landing page is attracting visitors but not qualified leads.
```

Process post:

```text
How to turn one service into a clear offer page.
```

## Limitations

Educational content should not become generic advice. It works best when tied to a real audience, a real decision, and a relevant offer.
