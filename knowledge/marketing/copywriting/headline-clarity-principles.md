# Headline Clarity Principles

## Summary

A headline should help the right person quickly understand relevance, outcome, and reason to continue reading.

It does not need to be clever. It needs to orient attention.

## Core Idea

Good headlines usually clarify at least two of these elements:

- audience;
- problem;
- desired outcome;
- category;
- mechanism;
- urgency;
- differentiation.

The more unfamiliar the offer, the more the headline must clarify category and outcome.

## When To Use

- Writing landing pages, sales pages, ads, social posts, email subjects, proposals, or PDF covers.
- Reviewing whether a page communicates value quickly.
- Turning vague copy into buyer-oriented messaging.

## When Not To Use

- When the audience already has strong context and a short label is enough.
- When legal, compliance, or brand constraints require a fixed title.
- When the issue is visual hierarchy rather than message clarity.

## How To Apply

Test a headline with five questions:

1. Who is this for?
2. What is being offered or explained?
3. What outcome or problem is implied?
4. Why should someone continue reading?
5. Could a stranger understand it in five seconds?

Useful patterns:

```text
[Outcome] for [audience]
How [audience] can [achieve outcome] without [pain]
The [category] for [audience] who need [specific result]
Stop [problem] before it causes [cost]
```

## Examples

Vague:

```text
Transform your digital presence.
```

Clearer:

```text
Websites for local service businesses that need more qualified WhatsApp leads.
```

## Limitations

Clarity does not mean flattening personality. Once relevance is clear, tone, rhythm, and brand voice can make the headline more memorable.
